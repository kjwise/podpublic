What Takes Form: The Perennial Contour

October 07, 2026Reading time: 12 min

A fish slips into a gap in a coral reef. The shelter it finds was made by other lives.

Stony coral polyps secrete skeletons of calcium carbonate. Colonies grow upon the remains of earlier colonies, building structures that become habitat for fish and other animals. Living activity makes a place in which further living activity becomes possible.1

The reef also changes the water moving across it. Its structure breaks waves and alters their passage. What grew through particular conditions now helps shape the conditions around it.2

We often tell the story of a thing by asking where it came from. Follow the reef a little longer and another question appears: What becomes possible because it is here?

The Perennial Contour follows a familiar relation through as many lenses as we can bring to it, seeking greater detail with each encounter:

What takes form affects the flow around it and can become part of the conditions through which further form becomes possible.

Here, form means an arrangement that holds together enough to be recognizable: a reef, an instrument, a learned skill. Flow means the ongoing activity through which such arrangements arise, persist, and change.

Perennial points to recurrence across times and ways of knowing. The claim is not that all traditions say the same thing, but that a relation recurs in what they differently say. Contour names the shape we are trying to discern within the movement: how flow takes form, how form redirects flow, and how the two remain involved in each other.

What the Flute Makes Possible

A reef shows how living activity can build a habitat. Human craft adds another possibility: something made can become a means of creating. Consider a wooden flute lying on a table.

Someone has hollowed and shaped the wood, cut an opening for the breath, and placed holes along its length. Those decisions shape how breath can become sound. With the instrument in hand, a player can produce phrases that an unworked piece of wood would not permit.

The breath drives a jet of air at the opening. Vibrations inside the tube deflect that jet, and the jet feeds energy back into the vibration. Opening or closing holes changes the tube’s resonances—the patterns of vibration it readily supports.3 Even a held note has to keep happening.

Having the flute is only part of being able to play. Making music requires the instrument, a body able to play it, and the skill to bring the two into relation. The flute can remain intact after everyone who knew its music has died. Keeping the object and keeping the capacity are different achievements.

Teaching carries something the object alone cannot. A teacher passes on how to hold the instrument, shape a note, and remember a phrase by ear. The learner can eventually vary the phrase, join another musician, or make something the teacher never imagined.

Through use and teaching, what has been made becomes part of the means of making something further. The instrument enters the making of music; the learned phrase enters the making of a new variation.

The flute demonstrates why limits can enable creativity. Its particular shape makes particular kinds of playing possible. Removing every limit would remove the instrument. Within its limits, however, many phrases remain unplayed. The craft gives breath a range without choosing the next tune.

An Evening Becomes a Tradition

Language carries this movement into shared life. Imagine a few neighbours bringing a table into a courtyard one Friday. Someone has cooked too much; someone else brings extra chairs. They eat until the light goes. A week later, a message appears: “Long table on Friday?”

The phrase means more now than it could have meant before that evening. People remember where to go, what to bring, whose shift ends late. An event has left them with a way to arrange another event.

At the next gathering, someone brings a board game. After the plates are cleared, they stay to play. A later invitation carries that possibility too. What they have done together changes what the invitation can open.

Here the form is distributed across language, expectation, objects, and people. The words help bring the gathering about; the gatherings teach people what the words mean. Keeping it alive involves shopping, making room, showing up, and changing the arrangement when lives change. The phrase is small enough to fit in a message. The history it carries is spread across several homes.

This is one way a culture grows: an expression enters shared use and becomes a means of doing something together.

Return Begins Before the Ending

The word return can suggest a final dissolution: a body returning to earth, a structure falling apart. Here it also names what happens when a form enters the activity through which further forms become possible. Something made returns to the making. It need not disappear to do so.

The reef is already changing its surroundings while it grows. A musician is already carrying a teacher’s work into another life while both are alive. Participation does not wait for disappearance. Formation and persistence have consequences of their own.

A phrase is recognizable as a whole, yet still has to be sustained from note to note.

The new whole remains dependent on what sustains it. Greater skill still needs fingers. A shared performance still needs the distinct musicians whose contributions make it possible. Their participation in the music does not exhaust who they are.

The same attention keeps an ending honest. A surviving instrument cannot restore the person who played it. A recording can carry the sound of someone whose next improvisation will never be heard. Something has passed onward; something has ended.

Continuation has several routes, and loss can close them. The fact that a form changes what follows gives no assurance that its capacities will be preserved. Sometimes what the next moment inherits is the absence of a possibility that was real.

Following the Contour

The reef grows without a maker planning it. The flute is deliberately crafted. What they share is a relation: produced structure becomes a condition of further activity. One makes habitat; the other enters music through learned use. The courtyard adds a form that exists across several participants: shared words and remembered occasions help people arrange their next meeting.

An explanation needs more than that resemblance. Suppose a learner’s variation carries a teacher’s phrase forward. We need a route of transmission and something identifiable that was retained or transformed. If the learner never encountered the phrase and developed the variation independently, the inheritance claim fails. The resemblance remains.

A damaging response can also be carried forward through what people learn in repeated encounters. Consider a couple after an angry outburst. “I’m sorry. I was exhausted,” one says. The other begins to explain what hurt: “When you shouted at me in front of our friends—”

“I’ve already apologized. Can we move on?”

The other person stops trying to finish the sentence. At the next dinner, another disagreement ends in shouting. On the way home comes another apology and the same demand to move on.

What carries forward is the expectation that an apology should close the subject.

A psychological account examines how this response was learned. A relational account asks what the apology changes between the people involved. A feedback account notices that the apology closes the discussion while leaving the conditions of the next outburst intact. Something produced by the pattern has become part of how it survives.

Following the relation makes a concrete question visible: what changes before the next outburst? The other person’s decision to stay, refuse these terms, or leave carries meanings and consequences that a diagram cannot exhaust.

These cases give me a reason to investigate a shared constraint, not yet evidence that one underlies them. My hypothesis is that their recurring structure belongs to the reality they share. Following it through science, art, myth, or ordinary experience means asking what each encounter adds—and allowing the answer to change the contour we thought we saw.

The accounts also meet inside events. The vibrating air in the flute is the sound through which a teacher’s work reaches a pupil. The pupil’s response changes what the teacher plays next. Physics, learning, and relationship follow different aspects of this exchange. Changing the instrument changes what can be taught; learning changes what can be played. Our accounts overlap because the activities themselves are meeting.

The Depth No Form Exhausts

What makes a world, with its laws and history, possible at all? The reef and the flute do not answer that question. Here I take a leap.

My wager is that reality is one continuous, inexhaustible movement of becoming. Flow takes form, form redirects flow, and every form participates in the movement it changes. Matter, life, consciousness, and whole worlds belong to this continuity.

Taking form and returning happen together. A form changes the activity through which it arises and endures; dissolution changes how that involvement continues. Return names this reciprocity at work throughout existence. It is already happening while a life is being lived.

Even a complete universe, with its laws and history, is one articulation of a depth that exceeds it. Its completion does not complete reality. Bounded Infinity names actual infinity expressing locally through finite form: a boundary gives depth a contour without exhausting it. A finished form can already hold more than we will survey.

Something can be new to us, or newly possible for us, without enlarging what reality permits. A finished room can remain unexplored; a room being built acquires walls, openings, and ways of being used through the building. The cases we have followed do not decide whether this world’s history is complete or still coming into being. I don’t know which room we’re in.

A world can depend on depth without that dependence deciding whether its possibilities are fixed. A fixed grammar can permit an endless succession of new expressions. I make a further wager about reality itself: no final grammar contains every way reality can take form. The universe we inhabit is real all the way through, and reality is deeper than the universe.

Law’s own ancestry poses a separate question: what establishes the constraints through which a world becomes possible, and what holds them in place? A law could itself have taken form within a wider order.

The Divine names this depth as sacred: the inexhaustible reality through which worlds and lives take form. Calling it Divine refuses to make the sacred a leftover that survives only where explanation fails. Understanding more of a form discloses more of the depth it expresses. You can follow the relation without accepting this account of depth or calling it Divine.

Inexhaustibility offers no bargain against mortality. The depth remains open while a life ends completely. A larger account that makes that loss unreal has ceased to attend to the life it claims to include.

Understanding Is Something That Happens

Light reaches your eyes. Your nervous system is already active, carrying a history that shapes what you notice and recognize. One stream meets another. Seeing is the encounter as it is lived from here.

At a crowded doorway, you turn sideways to pass. Someone sees the movement and draws back an elbow; the opening widens. What you saw changed how you moved, and your movement helped change what there was to see. Another person’s response enters the encounter while it is still unfolding. The crossing has a duration, and what happens within it changes its course.

Currents of one world cross within a particular life. The experience belongs to that life. Your grief remains yours; another person’s delight is their own.

Learning changes the next encounter. A listener learns to hear a musical interval. What once arrived as an indistinct movement of sound acquires a relation they can recognize, remember, and sing. That distinction can enter a melody of their own. Understanding has become one of the conditions through which something else can take form.

That listener hums a possible continuation before knowing quite how it will sound. The attempt gives them something new to hear. They adjust, try again, and discover a turn they would not have found by listening alone. Understanding has begun to invent its next encounter.

What a crossing changes can outlast the encounter itself. What was heard has become something the listener can attempt, recognize elsewhere, or offer to another person. The event is over; the capacity it helped form is now participating in other events.

No view from outside the world was required. Attention, memory, practice, and sound met within it. The understanding was an event in the reality it helped make legible. Inquiry has this intimacy: we are affected by what we learn. A concept can release attention or imprison it. A story can make an unfamiliar life imaginable, or train us to overlook the person in front of us.

Yet a pattern’s ability to continue does not make it worth continuing. Cruelty also acquires durable forms. The apology that closes discussion has served the pattern and failed the person who was hurt. Dignity, freedom, and care are commitments we bring to participation; no reef or recursive pattern can choose them for us. A stable pattern can deserve to be broken.

A Life Large Enough to Inhabit

We have to live while our understanding is still changing. Wholeness cannot require a finished account of everything that makes us possible. It takes shape as our different capacities learn to work together: desire beside judgement, grief alongside pleasure, a conviction held while another person’s answer is heard.

There are real achievements here. A skill becomes available. A conflict finds an ending. A person learns to love without having to make the beloved predictable. Further change need not revoke what has already become livable.

The cosmic question remains worth asking even when it gives us no instruction. What depth becomes partly visible through a form that cannot contain it? Such inquiry can accompany a life that also has hours for play, rest, and nothing that needs to become a lesson.

Someone lifts the flute from the table. The maker’s work is in the wood; the teacher’s patience is in the player’s hands. Much of what makes this moment possible lies beyond the player’s knowledge. A second musician begins, and a phrase the player knows finds an unexpected companion.

The inherited tune gives them somewhere to meet. What they make together has yet to be heard.
